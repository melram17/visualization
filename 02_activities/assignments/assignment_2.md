      Bad data viz example: https://viz.wtf/post/673200540513140736/time-dilation
      I classified the New York Times' "Time Dilation" graph, illustrating New Covid-19 cases in the United States, as poor example of data visualization for multiple compelling reasons. 
      
      Firstly, the graph omits its data sources. When dealing with public health information, the origin of the data is important context. Without knowing where this information was collected—whether from the CDC, state health departments, or other institutions—readers are left unable to verify its accuracy, understand potential biases, or assess the completeness of the dataset.
      
      Second is the graph's unconventional spiral structure, which hinders readability and comprehension. Unlike traditional time-series graphs where trends are immediately apparent through a simple left-to-right progression, this spiral design forces the viewer's eye into a curving path. This makes it challenging to follow the progression of Covid-19 cases over time, identify peaks or troughs, or overall trends with any ease. Furthermore, attempting to compare case numbers across different years or even months within the same year becomes a tough task, as the data points are not aligned in a way that facilitates direct comparison.
      
      Next, the quantification of Covid-19 cases is ambiguous, making it difficult for viewers to accurately interpret the scale. The radial lines indicating case numbers (from 0 to 150K) are not intuitively mapped onto the curved data path. This makes it hard to determine when thresholds, such as 150,000 cases, are surpassed.
      
      Finally, the "Time Dilation" graph presents significant accessibility issues. Its complex, non-linear layout is disorienting and difficult to track for individuals with dyslexia, certain visual processing disorders, or even just general cognitive load issues. Additionally, the non-standard visual would pose challenges for screen readers, effectively excluding visually impaired users from accessing the vital public health data it attempts to convey. In essence, the graph prioritizes a misguided attempt at artistic novelty over the fundamental principles of clear, accurate, and accessible data communication.
